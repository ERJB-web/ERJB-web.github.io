Analytical summary

Communicating science is not just a matter of transmitting facts

Science popularization transforms the form of a piece of knowledge without unduly changing its scope. It provides reference points for understanding a method, appraising the robustness of a finding and situating uncertainties. But perfectly accurate information is not necessarily understood, judged relevant or put to use.

Research on science communication studies the interactions between messages, audiences, institutions, media, decision situations and relationships of trust. It calls for defining the intended goal (understanding, dialogue, participation or use) before choosing the format. The National Academies point out that a one-way dissemination model does not meet every objective, particularly when values, collective consequences or controversies are at stake [1].

01 / Conceptualization

Popularization, dissemination, mediation, participation: distinct objectives

Science popularization aims to present knowledge, and the way it was produced, in terms a non-specialist audience can understand. It involves selection, prioritization, a change of register and often the use of examples. Its fidelity is measured not by the presence of every detail, but by respect for the findings, their degree of certainty and their conditions of validity.

Science communication is a broader category that includes exchanges between specialists, public dissemination, institutional communication and media information. Science mediation often adds a mechanism for appropriation: a visit, an exhibition, a dialogue with a researcher, a workshop or a participatory activity. Public engagement involves, to varying degrees, taking concerns into account and, sometimes, real influence over questions, methods or decisions. Knowledge mobilization concerns the conditions under which knowledge circulates and is used in action; implementation specifically examines the adoption and sustainability of practices.

02 / Frameworks

Three broad families of models and their limitations

The deficit model holds that difficulties of understanding or trust result mainly from a lack of knowledge. It can justify a useful educational explanation, but becomes inadequate when it presumes that transmitting facts automatically corrects attitudes. Dialogue models regard audiences as interlocutors whose expectations, knowledge and concerns shape the exchanges. Participation models go further when they provide for a power to act or decide. These models are not watertight historical periods: they coexist and can be combined depending on the problem.

The challenge is to define what one seeks to achieve without substituting a convenient indicator for the phenomenon under study. A video viewed by ten thousand people can have very wide reach without changing how a concept is interpreted. Conversely, a workshop bringing together a few participants can provide detailed knowledge of the obstacles to appropriation, without representing the general population.

03 / State of knowledge

What research allows us to assert and what remains debated

Effectiveness depends on the message, the situation and what is being measured

The National Academies synthesis (2017) relates audience characteristics (prior knowledge, numeracy, values, social belonging) to perceived credibility, the framing of information and the media environment [1]. It identifies an architecture of research questions more than a universal formula. Communication can contribute to understanding or support a decision without single-handedly determining its outcome. Exposure, endorsement and change in practice are distinct outcomes.

Trust is multidimensional

Cologna and colleagues distinguish competence, integrity, benevolence and openness to criticism, rather than treating trust as a single judgment. In their survey, perceived competence is on average higher than perceived openness to other points of view. The authors stress that responses vary across national contexts and that full metric comparability of the scales has not been established [3].

Correcting false information is not a “magic” intervention

Work on disinformation calls for distinguishing between a mistaken belief, the detection of incorrect information, stated intention and actual behaviour. In an experiment conducted in twelve countries, six inoculation strategies against climate disinformation messages provided only very little protection, whereas exposure to disinformation worsened several indicators. This result contradicts the idea that a short intervention can, on its own, neutralize misleading content in all contexts [6].

Digital media broaden formats, not necessarily social reach

Platforms make videos, podcasts, carousels, summaries and live sessions available. They also produce easy-to-track metrics (impressions, clicks, shares, comments) that do not assess understanding. Recommendation algorithms, language constraints and inequalities of access must be considered. The audiences reached by a post are not the same as all the audiences affected by a problem. The UNESCO Recommendation on Open Science establishes accessibility and participation as requirements broader than merely putting content online [5].

Methodological note.

Findings from randomized trials estimate the effects of specific messages in defined populations and conditions. Cross-sectional surveys describe distributions and associations. Institutional recommendations translate criteria and objectives; they do not, in themselves, constitute experimental evidence of the effectiveness of a particular strategy.

04 / Empirical data

Trust in science: a reading of international surveys

The Wellcome Global Monitor 2026 draws on more than 140,000 responses collected in 140 countries in 2025. To compare the 2018, 2020 and 2025 waves, the report uses the 108 countries present at all three time points. The proportion reporting “some” or “a lot” of trust in science rose from 77% in 2018 to 80% in 2020, then 72% in 2025. These figures should not be read without the report’s caveat: changes in survey mode, notably in China, affect comparisons over time [2].

The same report observes gaps linked to self-reported economic situation: 39% of people living comfortably say they have “a lot” of trust in science, compared with 20% among those experiencing financial difficulties. These comparisons describe an association, not a causal effect of affluence on trust. Similarly, 81% consider that scientists’ work benefits people like them, whereas only 37% judge that it benefits most people in their country [2].

Another survey, published in Nature Human Behaviour, collected responses from 71,922 people in 68 countries between November 2022 and August 2023. On a scale of trust in scientists ranging from 1 to 5, the aggregate mean is 3.62. The authors note that 78% consider scientists qualified to conduct important research, whereas 42% believe that scientists pay attention to other people’s views. This dissociation points to a difficulty of relationship and listening, not only of understanding [3].

Responsible communication should therefore not infer, from a global measure, that each population “believes” or “does not believe” science. The objects of trust, the wording of the question, timing and the concrete situation all matter.

05 / Experiments and meta-analyses

Uncertainty, consensus, disinformation: three experimental lessons

Making uncertainty visible

In four experiments and a field study, van der Bles and colleagues (2020) examined the reception of information featuring different ways of expressing uncertainty. The use of numerical ranges had an overall limited effect on reported trust. Verbal wording could produce different results depending on the indicators. These observations do not prove that every statement of uncertainty is without consequence: the topic, the institution speaking and the way limitations are presented remain essential [7].

Expressing scientific consensus

In a preregistered experiment conducted with 10,527 participants from online samples in 27 countries, a message recalling the scientific consensus on the human cause of climate change substantially reduced errors in estimating that consensus (standardized effect d = 0.47; 95% CI: 0.41–0.52). Direct changes in other attitudes, including support for public action, were, however, weaker or absent. An improvement in an indicator of understanding therefore does not mechanically imply a transformation of all behaviours [8].

Responding to false information

The meta-analysis by Chan and Albarracín (2023) examines the effects of corrections on false beliefs about scientific issues; the authors deal with a heterogeneous literature whose effects are moderated by the characteristics of the interventions and of the beliefs [9]. A later analysis conducted in twelve countries, with 6,816 participants, shows that several attempts at psychological prevention offered only very little protection against misleading climate content, under the experimental conditions studied [6]. This contrast invites us not to confuse correction after exposure, prevention, understanding of consensus and lasting action.

06 / Writing method

Turning a finding into a rigorous explanation

Good popularization does not begin with the search for a metaphor. It begins by identifying the claim that withstands scrutiny of the study. One must make explicit what is known, how it is known and to whom the finding may apply. The final wording can claim causality only if the study design and the body of arguments justify it; hypothesis, association and recommendation remain distinct intellectual acts.

01

Qualify the source

Original article? Review? Protocol? Report? Preprint? Assess the design and the status.

02

Check the measure

Population, date, denominator, effect size, uncertainty interval and bias.

03

Write for an audience

Concrete question, explained term, main finding, faithful illustration, nuance.

04

Test and correct

Rephrasing by the audience, accessibility, link checking, dated correction.

The CDC offers a structured grid of 20 criteria covering, among other things, the visibility of the main message, plain language, visual organization, the state of knowledge, numbers and risk communication. This Clear Communication Index provides a framework for editorial review. It cannot replace an evaluation with real audiences [4].

Example applied to primary care

Inadequate message: “A dense network of partners guarantees integrated care.” This wording turns a hypothesis into a certainty. A more accurate statement would be: “The presence of many partners is not enough to establish that people receive a coordinated response. Actual relationships, trajectories and the experience of the people concerned must also be studied.” The second text distinguishes the configuration of the network from the observable quality of the response. It does not present as established thesis findings that have yet to be produced.

07 / Public responsibility

Accessibility, literacy and equity

Making content available does not mean making it usable. Obstacles relate to language, disability, health literacy, available time, material conditions of access, familiarity with institutions and cultural representations. An undifferentiated “general public” strategy can increase visibility among people already familiar with research, while leaving out those who most need the information.

The 2021 UNESCO Recommendation conceives of open science as a set of practices aimed at accessibility, reuse and opening the scientific process to actors beyond the research community. It calls for consideration of inequalities of access and multilingual contexts [5]. For digital materials, the W3C recommends, among other things, verified captions, transcripts and, where necessary, descriptions of visual elements [10].

A sound evaluation defines the unit of measurement and the expected change in advance. Five levels can be distinguished: production (materials actually disseminated), reach (people actually reached), understanding (what people are able to interpret), participation (who was able to contribute) and use (documented decisions or practices). Progress on one of these indicators does not automatically validate the others.

For evaluations of understanding, an open-ended question or an application to a new situation can be more informative than a simple satisfaction survey. For participatory formats, it is essential to report back to people what was retained, rejected or left pending.

09 / Limitations and ethics

Making knowledge accessible without building false certainty

The most common pitfalls involve selecting only spectacular findings, presenting associations as causes, generalizing to different audiences, using illustrations far removed from the data, concealing limitations and confusing media interest with scientific quality. A graph can be arithmetically accurate yet misleading through its scale, its categories or the period selected.

Transparency requires keeping links to sources, dating the data, separating commentary from findings, disclosing relevant funding and conflicts of interest, and correcting errors publicly. The humanities, social sciences and public health also require distinguishing between a participant’s words, the researcher’s interpretation and a population-level conclusion.

10 / Perspectives

A question of the quality of the relationship between science and society

The studies presented show that we must move beyond the artificial opposition between a complex science reserved for specialists and a popularization that is necessarily simplistic. Accessible content can rigorously address hypotheses, methods and disagreements. The challenge is not to systematically produce more information, but to better explain why it is produced, what it makes possible and where uncertainty begins.

Several lines of work remain open: comparing long-term understanding across formats; measuring the real effect of an exchange with audiences rather than their satisfaction alone; studying people who are under-represented in surveys; understanding how institutional trust relates to experiences of services; and evaluating the impact of error-correction mechanisms beyond stated intentions. These questions call for suitable designs, sometimes mixed, and a responsibility shared between research, institutions and the communities concerned.

Finally, for public health and primary care, popularizing accurately means not confusing explaining a complex situation, advocating a response and demonstrating its effectiveness. These are three complementary tasks, but methodologically different ones.

11 / Quebec and Canadian contexts

Science mediation adapted to local practices and institutions

To design popularization activities in Quebec, data on media use must be distinguished from data that genuinely concern scientific understanding. The Institut de la statistique du Québec surveyed about 16,000 people aged 15 and over in 2024 about their cultural leisure activities. The results indicate that 96% use the Internet for personal purposes, 89% are active on social media and 49% listen to podcasts. These measures describe general practices: they demonstrate neither that these audiences consult scientific content nor that they retain its messages [13].

Differences between groups matter for the choice of materials. According to the same survey, podcast listening reaches 61% among people living in a high-income household compared with 39% for those living in a low-income household; this contrast does not say why uses differ. Consequently, a dissemination policy relying exclusively on podcasts risks not reaching all audiences evenly, even when digital access is high.

The Public Health Agency of Canada’s Stratégie scientifique 2024-2025 à 2029-2030 explicitly identifies communication science as a priority distinct from implementation science. It calls for explaining uncertainties, strengthening transparency, adapting resources to community needs and addressing disinformation without losing sight of the imperatives of equity and reconciliation [14]. In Quebec, the tenth Forum of the Fonds de recherche du Québec (November 2024) brought together nearly 300 people around scientific trust and the science–society dialogue. This forum illustrates an institutional space for reflection; its attendance is not a representative estimate of public opinion [15].
