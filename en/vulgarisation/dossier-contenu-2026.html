Science popularization is not simply a matter of making complex knowledge easier to understand. It is a set of mediation, communication and engagement practices that seek to make scientific knowledge accessible, intelligible, open to discussion and usable in society.

Contemporary work invites us to move beyond a strictly top-down conception of popularization. It highlights the importance of dialogue, trust, social contexts, accessibility and public participation in the circulation of knowledge (National Academies, 2017).

1. Understanding the field: five complementary conceptions

The literature makes it possible to distinguish several purposes that are sometimes grouped under the single term “science popularization” (National Academies, 2017).

ApproachMain purposeExample
PopularizationExplaining concepts or findingsVideo on a research method
Science communicationCirculating reliable, contextualized informationArticle, infographic, podcast
Science mediationBuilding bridges between knowledge and audiencesInteractive exhibition or workshop
Science–society dialogueEnabling questions, experiences and perspectives to be brought togetherScience café, citizen meeting
Engagement and participationInvolving audiences in a scientific reflection or processParticipatory science, co-design

These categories overlap. Here they constitute an analytical synthesis grid rather than a universally accepted classification.

From the deficit model to the participatory model

The so-called deficit model assumes that difficulties in the reception of science stem mainly from a lack of knowledge. The US National Academies have highlighted the limitations of this conception: providing more information is not necessarily enough to change an attitude or a behaviour, since values, experiences, identities and relationships of trust also come into play (National Academies, 2017).

Synthesis diagram: three logics of communication
1. Transmission: scientist → publicKnowledge is essentially transmitted in one direction.
2. Dialogue: scientist ↔ publicQuestions, interpretations and experiences flow in both directions.
3. Participation: scientists + citizens + field actors → Collective construction of a project or of knowledgeThe various actors can help define the question, the procedures and the uses.

Original synthesis diagram, based on the approaches examined by the National Academies (National Academies, 2017). It does not represent a mandatory progression: each approach can be relevant depending on the objective.

The real challenge is therefore to adapt the communication approach to its objective: making a finding known, improving its understanding, supporting a discussion, helping to make a decision or organizing genuine participation.

2. What recent surveys show

2.1 A demand for science, but contrasting relationships with information

The Baromètre de l’esprit critique 2026, conducted for Universcience, provides particularly interesting results. The survey includes, among others, a sample of 2,000 French adults and another of 609 people aged 15 to 24, constructed using the quota method (Universcience, 2026).

Its results show that interest in science remains strong, but that trust in institutions, access to information and participation in science activities are not evenly distributed across social groups (Universcience, 2026).

These data qualify the idea that young people are interested in science only through short videos. Engagement also comes through museums, experimentation and direct exchanges (Universcience, 2026).

Another finding deserves attention: in 2026, 48% of the French adults surveyed believe that the scientific community is independent in validating its results, while 62% consider that scientists follow strict ethical rules (Universcience, 2026). Interest in science and trust in its institutions must therefore be distinguished.

2.2 Where do audiences encounter scientific knowledge?

A study published in 2025 in Science Communication, based on 71,922 people surveyed in 68 countries, compared different channels of exposure to science information (Mede et al., 2025).

The value of this study lies in showing the multiplicity of pathways to science. Social media play a central role, but news media, everyday conversations and institutional mediation formats remain important (Mede et al., 2025). The predominance of a channel does not mean that it produces better understanding.

3. Popularization formats: what to choose and why?

The literature does not justify declaring any one format (video, text, podcast or exhibition) universally superior. The choice must be tied to the nature of the knowledge, the audience, the context of use and the intended outcome (National Academies, 2017). This is notably the logic recommended by the National Academies (National Academies, 2017) and the CDC (CDC, n.d.).

An effective editorial architecture offers several levels: a short presentation, an in-depth explanation, then access to the data and sources. This lets readers choose their level of engagement without sacrificing scientific accuracy.

4. What is known about the effectiveness of methods?

It is useful to distinguish a recommendation grounded in research from a practice that is merely popular. Experimental studies provide valuable knowledge, but their effectiveness depends on the situation.

ApproachState of knowledgePractical implication
Stories and storytellingStories can foster interest, understanding and engagement, but also carry a persuasive dimension (Dahlstrom, 2014).Use a story to introduce a mechanism, without substituting anecdote for data.
Communicating uncertaintyExperiments show that numerical uncertainty ranges generally have only small negative effects on trust (van der Bles et al., 2020).Present margins of error and limitations rather than artificial certainties.
Preventing disinformationSix randomized trials and a field study found favourable effects of short videos explaining manipulation techniques (Roozenbeek et al., 2022).Teach how to recognize misleading reasoning, not just correct a claim.
Plain-language communicationThe CDC offers 20 documented criteria covering the message, words, structure, numbers and risks (CDC, n.d.).Systematically check readability and clarity, then test with audiences.
Community dialogueThe OMS recommends listening and participation mechanisms to understand needs and co-construct appropriate responses (OMS, 2025).Plan for questions, feedback and changes, rather than exclusively top-down dissemination.
The essential case of scientific uncertainty

Good popularization distinguishes three levels:

What research establishesSufficiently supported findings, with an indication of their scope and context.
What remains uncertainMethodological limitations, divergence between studies, quality and quantity of evidence.
What is still under studyHypotheses and questions that require further research, without presenting them as established discoveries.

In June 2025, the OMS published a guide specifically devoted to communicating uncertainty in health emergencies (OMS Europe, 2025). Although this context is specific, its principles of transparency and trust are useful in communicating scientific findings.

Three particularly useful institutional tools
CDC: Clear Communication Index

A grid of 20 scored criteria for examining public health content. The CDC recommends a score of at least 90/100, an independent review and a pretest with the target audience (CDC, n.d.). The tool also applies to formats such as infographics and podcast scripts.

Complete guide and downloadable grids
NCCPE: Evaluation 101

A method for evaluating public engagement activities organized around four operations: building a theory of change, choosing the outcomes to measure, defining the level of evidence and selecting the instruments (NCCPE, 2024).

Reference framework for digital accessibility: contrast, alternative text, captions, keyboard navigation, adaptable presentation and legibility of graphs (W3C, 2023). These requirements are particularly relevant for older audiences or those with sensory limitations.

6. How can the effectiveness of popularization be measured?

The number of views, “likes” or shares mainly provides information on dissemination and certain forms of interaction. It does not automatically demonstrate knowledge acquisition, trust, a change in practices or a social effect (NCCPE, 2024).

A rigorous evaluation must distinguish outputs, reception, learning and impacts.

The recent 68-country research itself stresses the need to better understand differences between audiences, platforms and national contexts. It also recommends going beyond cross-sectional studies alone to better assess causal mechanisms (Mede et al., 2025).

8. Application: popularizing research on home support

For research on older adults, social needs and the intersectoral organization of services, popularization can go well beyond a summary of the protocol.

Example of an editorial pathway
Start from a concrete situationAn older adult living at home needs several forms of help, but does not always know which organizations to turn to. The problem is presented without claiming that one individual experience represents all cases.
Visualize a territoryA map explains the geographic distribution of identified resources, the limits of data coverage and the distinction between the presence of a service and actual accessibility.
Help people understand a networkA graph represents the relationships observed between organizations, with a legend specifying what the nodes, ties and measures mean. It must avoid attributing relationships that have not been documented.
Open the discussionA short video clip or a workshop allows the people concerned, caregivers and practitioners to comment on the interpretations and point out missing information.

In this context, popularization can play a role complementary to knowledge transfer and implementation: it makes it possible to explain, to raise questions and to open findings to discussion, without presuming that they will be immediately adopted in practice.

The CRSH knowledge mobilization guide is useful for distinguishing dissemination, exchange, co-production and use of findings in a research project (CRSH, n.d.).

10. Proposed organization of the dossier on a scientific website

To turn this knowledge into a genuinely consultable resource, I would favour an organization into six distinct spaces, rather than concentrating all the documents and visuals on a single dense page.

01 Understand

Definitions, models, historical landmarks

02 State of knowledge

Studies, statistics, graphs and reports

03 Formats and productions

Videos, podcasts, infographics and examples

04 Methods and tools

Guides, grids, diagrams and good practices

05 Ongoing research

Projects, controversies and open questions

06 Participate and evaluate

Quizzes, dialogue and feedback

This structure would provide a library of genuinely consultable scientific resources: figures attributed to their publications, statistics accompanied by their methodology, original media, educational diagrams and tools for checking understanding.
